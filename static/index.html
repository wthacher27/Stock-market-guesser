How it works

Each model predicts tomorrow's percent return, not the raw price. The forecast is today's close moved by that return. Predicting returns stops a model from just memorizing that the index sits near 7,000.

The features are what's known at today's close: the last five daily returns, the distance from the 5, 10, 20, 50 and 200-day moving averages, 10- and 30-day volatility, 14-day RSI, the day's high–low range, and volume relative to its 20-day average.

Every model's raw forecast is then calibrated. Each model holds back the most recent fifth of its training data, checks how its forecasts did there, and learns how much to trust them. Untrustworthy forecasts get pulled toward the market's typical small daily rise, but every model keeps at least a quarter of its own opinion, so the bots don't all turn into the same guess. On the practice backtest this took the models beating No Change from none of 8 to all 8.

Naive Bayes is a classifier, so it sorts days into five return buckets (big down … big up). Its forecast is each bucket's average return, weighted by how likely the model thinks that bucket is.

The models retrain on every new close using all the saved data, back to 2005. Across five two-year test periods, that beat training on only the last 5 or 10 years: short windows learned a calm market and got burned in 2022. Each round's forecasts are frozen when the round opens. You can't see them, or the other players' median, until you lock in your own guess.

Every visitor gets one guess per trading day, and every guess is saved. Humanity's guess for the day is the median of all of them, so a few deliberately terrible guesses can't move it. Humanity and your own guess (You) compete as separate entrants against the bots.

Daily index moves are close to a coin flip, so the hard part is beating No Change. If a model beats it by even a few percent over hundreds of days, that's doing well.
